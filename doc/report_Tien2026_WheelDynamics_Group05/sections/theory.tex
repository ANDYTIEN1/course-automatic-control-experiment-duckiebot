\section{Theory}
\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{First-order model of a DC motor driving a wheel}
A permanent-magnet DC motor converts the armature voltage $V$ into torque. When the armature inductance is neglected, the armature current is $i=(V-k_e\omega)/R$, where $R$ is the armature resistance, $k_e$ the back-EMF constant, and $\omega$ the wheel angular speed. The rotating mass then obeys
\begin{equation}
J\dot{\omega} = k_t i - b\omega - T_f,
\label{eq:motor}
\end{equation}
where $J$ is the moment of inertia, $k_t$ the torque constant, $b$ the viscous friction coefficient, and $T_f$ the Coulomb (dry) friction torque. The motor driver applies the PWM duty cycle $u$, so the average voltage is $V=uV_s$ with supply voltage $V_s$.

If dry friction is ignored ($T_f=0$), substituting $i$ into \eqref{eq:motor} gives the linear first-order model \parencite{franklin2019FeedbackControlDynamic}
\begin{equation}
\tau\dot{\omega}(t)+\omega(t)=G\,u(t),
\qquad
\frac{\Omega(s)}{U(s)}=\frac{G}{\tau s+1},
\label{eq:firstorder}
\end{equation}
with gain and time constant
\begin{equation}
G=\frac{k_tV_s}{bR+k_tk_e},
\qquad
\tau=\frac{JR}{bR+k_tk_e}.
\label{eq:params}
\end{equation}
Both $G$ and $\tau$ depend only on constant physical parameters. The model therefore predicts the same gain and time constant at every operating point.

\subsection{Step response}
For a step in duty from $u_0$ to $u_1$ at $t=0$, starting from the steady speed $\omega_0=Gu_0$, the solution of \eqref{eq:firstorder} is
\begin{equation}
\omega(t)=\omega_0+G\,(u_1-u_0)\left(1-e^{-t/\tau}\right),\qquad t\ge 0.
\label{eq:step}
\end{equation}
The speed reaches 63.2\% of its total change at $t=\tau$ and never overshoots its final value. We characterise measured responses with the rise time $t_r$ (10\% to 90\% of the change), the maximum overshoot $M_p$, and the settling time $t_s$ (time after which the speed stays within $\pm1\%$ of its final value). For the model in \eqref{eq:step}, $t_r=\tau\ln 9\approx2.2\tau$, $M_p=0$, and $t_s=\tau\ln 100\approx4.6\tau$.

A delay between the command and the start of the response, for example in the driver, is modelled by a dead time $\theta\ge0$:
\begin{equation}
\omega(t)=
\begin{cases}
\omega_0, & t<\theta,\\
\omega_0+G\,(u_1-u_0)\left(1-e^{-(t-\theta)/\tau}\right), & t\ge\theta.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:fopdt}
\end{equation}
The ideal model \eqref{eq:step} is the special case $\theta=0$.

\subsection{Predictions tested in this experiment}
The linear model \eqref{eq:firstorder} leads to three predictions.
\begin{description}
\item[P1 (no dead zone).] Any nonzero step moves the wheel: the steady speed is $\omega_\infty=Gu$, so the wheel rotates even at small $|u|$.
\item[P2 (constant parameters).] $G$ and $\tau$ are the same for every step size and operating point, for example for the steps $0\to0.6$ and $0.6\to1.0$.
\item[P3 (symmetry, no hysteresis).] A step down is the mirror image of the step up, with the same $\tau$, and the duty at which the wheel starts equals the duty at which it stops.
\end{description}

\subsection{Expected deviations}
Dry friction breaks these predictions \parencite{armstronghelouvry1994SurveyModelsAnalysis}. With $T_f\neq0$ in \eqref{eq:motor}, the wheel stays at rest until the motor torque exceeds the static (breakaway) friction, which produces a dead zone around $u=0$ (against P1). Because static friction is usually larger than kinetic friction, a rotating wheel keeps moving at duties below the one needed to start it, which produces hysteresis (against P3). Friction and back-EMF effects that vary with speed, together with saturation of the driver near full duty, make the apparent $G$ and $\tau$ depend on the operating point (against P2).
