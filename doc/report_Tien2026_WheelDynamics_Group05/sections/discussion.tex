\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Physical interpretation and model validity}
The low-duty response and the different starting and stopping duties
(Figs.~\ref{fig:g5-small-duty} and~\ref{fig:g5-hysteresis}) indicate that
the wheel's response depended on its motion history. A plausible explanation
is that the drive torque had to overcome static friction before motion
began, while a smaller torque could sustain rotation once the wheel was
moving. This interpretation is consistent with the distinction between
static and sliding friction discussed by
\textcite{armstronghelouvry1994SurveyModelsAnalysis}. The experiment measured
wheel motion and commanded duty, so it did not identify friction torque
directly. The detected onset also depended on the sampled duty levels and
the motion threshold $\OmegaMin$ rad/s. The reported starting and stopping
duties therefore describe the tested sweep and detection rule, with limited
resolution near each transition.

The first-order model captured the main shape of individual step responses,
especially the acceleration from rest to 0.6 duty
(Fig.~\ref{fig:g5-step-06}). Its residual RMS was $\UpSixRms\%$ of the
speed change. However, extrapolating this fit to full duty overestimated
the final speed (Fig.~\ref{fig:g5-step-10}), and the fitted gain and time
constant changed with the operating point (Table~\ref{tab:g5steps}).
The gain fitted over a duty increment describes the speed change per unit
change in duty over that interval. It need not equal the ratio of final
speed to final duty when the steady response is nonlinear. Thus the fits
provide empirical descriptions of the tested transitions, while a single
pair of constant parameters did not describe the full command range.
Possible contributors include friction, changes in effective motor voltage
under load, and driver behaviour. Voltage and current were not recorded,
so these mechanisms cannot be distinguished from these measurements.

The transient asymmetry provides a further limit on a common linear model.
The time constant for starting was $\StartStopRatio$ times that for
stopping, whereas the decrease from full duty to 0.6 was slower than the
reverse increase by a factor of $\DownUpRatio$
(Table~\ref{tab:g5steps}). This reversal of the ordering shows that a
single rule that acceleration is always slower than deceleration would
also be insufficient. Adding a fitted delay left the residual RMS for the
step to 0.6 unchanged at the reported precision
($\UpSixRmsD\%$), so a fixed dead time alone did not explain the observed
departures from the model.

\subsection{Measurement limitations and scope}
The quality report for \texttt{db17-r5-right-m10} contained no warnings,
but it noted that the positive sweep's steady-part angle scatter was
8.98 times the pixel-based uncertainty, indicating tracking errors.
This limits confidence in the precise low-duty transition levels.
The step-response run had a lower scatter factor of $\KETwo$ and no
reported dropped frames.

These processing choices affect the timing and the speed estimates as follows. The options \texttt{-{}-wheel-follow} and \texttt{-{}-led-follow} only move the search regions; the angle is still computed from the marker and hub centroids, so they add no error of their own, and any tracking error that remains shows in the angle scatter $k$, which was $\KETwo$ for E2 and $\KEOne$ for the decreasing E1 sweep but 8.98 for the increasing sweep. Because the motion threshold $\omega_{\min} = \OmegaMin$~rad/s was set from the speeds measured at rest in the same videos, this scatter is included in the threshold that defines the start and stop duties. Every trial was synchronised by the log and the LED, each E1 video by its own LED switches, so splitting E1 into two videos added no timing error; synchronising by the onset of motion instead would have shifted the E1 runs by 0.6 to 0.9~s, which is why the LED tracking was needed. For E2, the LED and the log agreed within 3.0~ms (median 1.1~ms), and the timing uncertainty of $\TimingSd$~ms is below the frame interval of 8.3~ms; it is part of the spread over the three repetitions that the reported uncertainties of $G$ and $\tau$ are based on. The time stamps of the left-wheel videos were scaled by exactly 1/8, the ratio of the capture rate to the playback rate, without re-encoding, so this correction adds no uncertainty; the left wheel is not used in the numbers reported here.

The first repetition of the step-response experiment had an LED timing
uncertainty of $\TimingSd$ ms. The angular velocity was estimated using
a weighted local quadratic fit with a half-window of 23 ms.
Both the timing uncertainty and the temporal resolution of the
differentiation method limit the interpretation of short transient
responses. In particular, the fitted delays of approximately 0.9 ms
for the step from rest to 0.6 duty and 1.5 ms for the step from full
duty to 0.6 are smaller than the LED timing uncertainty.
These small delays should therefore not be interpreted as reliable
evidence of physical motor dead time.

The angular velocity uncertainty was propagated from the angle
measurement uncertainty through the covariance matrix of the
weighted local quadratic fit. For the step from duty 0.6 to 1.0,
the median standard uncertainty of angular velocity was
$\OmegaSd$ rad/s.

The RMS angular velocity uncertainties for the four duty transitions
from 0 to 0.6, 0.6 to 1.0, 1.0 to 0.6, and 0.6 to 0 were
approximately 0.37, 0.36, 0.36, and 0.32 rad/s, respectively.
These correspond to $\UpSixNoiseRms\%$, $\UpTenNoiseRms\%$,
$\DownSixNoiseRms\%$, and $\DownZeroNoiseRms\%$ of their
respective speed changes. The smaller speed changes between
0.6 and full duty result in larger relative uncertainties,
even though the absolute angular velocity uncertainties are similar.

The residuals contain contributions from both model mismatch and
measurement uncertainty. For the step to full duty, the residual RMS was
$\UpTenRms\%$ of the speed change, compared with a propagated speed
uncertainty RMS of $\UpTenNoiseRms\%$ on the same scale.
Consequently, the residual magnitude alone cannot assign every
fluctuation to motor dynamics. The angular velocity uncertainty
also limits the accuracy with which the transient response and
steady-state speed can be determined, and should be considered
when interpreting the fitted gain and time constant.

Furthermore, the RMS angular velocity uncertainties exceed the
corresponding 1\% settling-band widths for all four duty transitions.
This makes it difficult to distinguish measurement fluctuations
from actual deviations of the wheel speed from its final value.
The unresolved settling times in Table~\ref{tab:g5steps} therefore
reflect the analysis's noise and settling-band criterion.
Substituting settling times calculated from the fitted model
would conceal that measurement limitation.

These conclusions apply to motor m10 on R5's right wheel in the stand
configuration, with three repetitions of the step sequence and one sweep
in each direction. The missing wheel-radius measurements prevent a
radius-based conversion to wheel-rim speed, and the experiment did not
establish ground speed, slip, or behaviour for other motors. A controller
based on these results would need validation at its intended operating
point, particularly near starting and stopping, where motion history
mattered.
