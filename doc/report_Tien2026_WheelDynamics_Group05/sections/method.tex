\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Apparatus}
We tested the right drive motor, m10, of Duckiebot DB17 robot R5. A Raspberry Pi drove the motor through a PCA9685-based Adafruit DC \& Stepper Motor HAT at a nominal PWM frequency of 1600~Hz. The setup was powered by an RPi UPSPack Standard Li-polymer battery; its voltage was not measured. The Pi logged every motor command with its time stamp.

A blue marker tape was attached from the hub to the rim of the yellow wheel. The wheel was filmed side-on with an iPhone in slow-motion mode at 1920$\times$1080 pixels and 120 frames per second, so the frame interval, and hence the time resolution, was 8.3~ms.
A green LED connected to GPIO21 was kept in the camera view. The LED switched just before each motor command, which allowed the video to be synchronised with the command log.

\subsection{Experiments}
\paragraph{E1: Duty sweep.} After 2~s at zero duty, the duty was raised from 0.10 to 0.36 in steps of 0.02 and then lowered back to 0.10, with each level held for 2~s so that the wheel could reach a steady speed. The sweep was run once with positive and once with negative duty.
E1 was used to find the start and stop duties (P1, P3).

\paragraph{E2: Step sequence.} The duty followed the sequence $0\to0.6\to1.0\to0.6\to0$, with each level held for 3~s.
The sequence was repeated three times. E2 was used to estimate $G$ and $\tau$ at two operating points (P2) and to compare upward and downward steps (P3).

\subsection{Data processing}
The videos were analysed with the course scripts. \texttt{track\_video.py} tracked the marker, the hub, and the LED in every frame. \texttt{process\_raw.py} synchronised the frames with the command log using the LED and computed the wheel angle, angular speed, and input, with their uncertainties. The angle uncertainty was derived from the pixel resolution of the tracked marker. \texttt{quality\_report.py} checked each run; a warning was raised when, for example, the scatter of the steady angle was much larger than its pixel-based uncertainty.

Because the camera drifted by up to about 100~px during recording and the robot vibrated on the stand, fixed search regions missed the LED and located the marker on the laptop screen in the background. The videos were therefore tracked with the options \texttt{-{}-wheel-follow 150} and \texttt{-{}-led-follow 150}, which let the search regions of the hub, the marker, and the LED follow that drift from start positions read from the first frame (\texttt{-{}-hub-region}, \texttt{-{}-marker-region}, \texttt{-{}-led-xy}). E1 was filmed as two videos, one per sweep direction, and analysed as one sweep. All data were processed with the scripts at commit \href{https://github.com/ANDYTIEN1/course-automatic-control-experiment-duckiebot/commit/e4c44bb}{\texttt{e4c44bb}} of our group repository.

For E1, the steady speed at each duty level was summarised as the mean $\pm$ standard deviation of the speeds over the last 0.8~s of the level.
The wheel was classified as moving when the magnitude of its mean speed exceeded the motion threshold $\omega_{\min}$, defined as the speed magnitude exceeded by only 1\% of the speeds measured with the wheel at rest before each trial; this gave 0.69~rad/s.
The start duty was the first level in the increasing sweep at which motion was detected, and the stop duty was the first level in the decreasing sweep at which the wheel was at rest again.

For E2, the first-order step response \eqref{eq:step} without a delay term was fitted by least squares to the three repetitions of each step together, giving $G$ and $\tau$; their spread is the standard deviation of the values fitted to each repetition separately. The fit was repeated with a dead time $\theta$ added to the model to test for a delay. The rise time, overshoot, and settling time were computed from the response averaged over the three repetitions, as defined in Section~\ref{sec:theory}. The fitted models were extrapolated to predict the speed at other duties and compared with the measurements.
